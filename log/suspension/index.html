A bandpass filter lets a small region of pitches through. Here, three filters wander together between 100 and 400 Hz. When they meet a harmonic the wire can sustain, it grows louder. The filters narrow and slow down to stay with it; when it fades, they open and search again.

The filters do not generate the notes. They change how much of the wire's own vibration returns. Higher Q means a narrower window. Brightness gives one part of the range more weight, while automatic gain control keeps a strong resonance from taking over.

About this sound model

This is a condensed demonstration of the v7.0.4 firmware, including energy memory, adaptive Q and movement, resonance holds, gain normalization, rescue, and automatic breaths. It does not reproduce the later v8 long-term gain calibration or every physical interaction. A lossy digital string replaces the physical wire, pickup, and coil. Its coupling and level control are calibrated for this browser demonstration; its sound is illustrative. The recording below is the real installation.

Inside the loop.

I had the pickup and driver coils custom made for this installation. Each wire has its own preamp, Daisy Seed, and power amplifier. Follow the signal from the wire and back again.

Hear the wire without touching it.

As the steel wire moves through the pickup's magnetic field, it changes the magnetic flux through the coil. The coil turns that motion into a small voltage, ready for the preamp.

The custom H1916 pickup is 19 mm across and 16 mm deep, with a 10 mm iron core and an M3 mounting hole. Its fine 42 AWG copper winding is specified at 575 Ω ±10%.

I requested a small N52 magnet inside, epoxy around the winding, and two versions with opposite winding directions and magnet polarities. The requested inductance was 1.2–1.8 H; that is an order specification, not a measured result.

Give a quiet signal room to move.

The pickup's signal is too small to use directly. The OPA2134PA raises its voltage eleven times while its JFET input places very little load on the coil. A 100 kΩ feedback resistor and 10 kΩ resistor set that gain: 1 + 100/10 = 11.

The board runs from a single 12 V supply. Two 10 kΩ resistors create a midpoint near 6 V, and the unused half of the OPA2134 buffers it. This gives the audio room to swing both above and below a steady centre. A 1 MΩ input resistor holds the pickup signal around that reference.

Why the extra parts?

220 Ω + 100 pF at the input
Reduce radio-frequency interference before it reaches the amplifier.
100 nF film capacitors
Couple audio into and out of the preamp while keeping the DC bias on its own side of the circuit.
100 pF across the feedback resistor
Reduces gain at very high frequencies to help the amplifier stay stable.
47 Ω + 47 μF, with 100 nF nearby
Filter the analogue supply and provide local charge when the op-amp needs it.
100 Ω output resistor, 100 kΩ bleed, SS210 diodes
Isolate the output, establish its DC level, and clamp excursions at the Daisy input.

The sensitive input trace is only 2.92 mm long. Analogue and power grounds meet at one 0 Ω link, and the power amplifier stays on a separate board. These choices reduce unwanted coupling into a signal that will pass around the feedback loop again and again. A Murata OKI-78SR-5 converter supplies the Daisy's 5 V rail; five Bourns PTV09A 10 kΩ controls connect to its control inputs.

Daisy Seed

The Daisy turns the pickup voltage into digital samples 48,000 times a second. Three filters sit inside a larger adaptive process. The v7.0.4 program follows this sequence continuously:

  1. Prepare the incoming signal.

    Pickup gain brings up the level. An 80 Hz high-pass filter removes slow movement and an 8 kHz low-pass filter removes very high frequencies from the feedback path.

  2. Let three regions through.

    Three bandpass filters move as a loose cluster within 100–400 Hz. Their outputs are added together. Harmonics near their centres receive more support on the next trip around the loop.

  3. Listen to what caught.

    An energy follower measures the filtered signal. It compares the current level with a slowly changing, 20-second average. A growing resonance narrows the filters from a broad Q of 1.5 toward 8 and slows their wandering, with a short hold so it can settle.

  4. Balance the returning energy.

    Intensity sets the feedback amount. As the signal rises above its usual level, gain normalization turns the feedback down. Brightness moves a broad emphasis between 80 and 300 Hz so the highest harmonics do not always win.

  5. Keep the sound moving.

    If the wire goes quiet, a rescue routine opens the filters and boosts the loop until a resonance catches. If it stays on one sound too long, an automatic breath fades the drive, shifts the filters, and lets another harmonic grow. A brief Q boost helps that new resonance settle.

  6. Send it back to the wire.

    A DC blocker removes offset. A soft limiter rounds large peaks, followed by a final ±0.95 ceiling. Driver gain and polarity set the outgoing signal; the converter returns it to voltage for the amplifier. The coil moves the wire, the pickup senses that motion, and the cycle starts again.

A separate output carries the raw pickup signal to the room speakers. The audience hears the vibrating wire; the filtered signal does the work of sustaining it.

Turn voltage back into motion.

The Daisy sends an audio signal to a TPA3118 Class-D amplifier. The amplifier supplies the current the coil needs. It stays on a separate board, away from the sensitive pickup input.

The permanent magnet supplies a steady magnetic field. Current in the winding changes that field with the audio signal, changing the force on the nearby steel wire. With the right spacing and timing, each pass adds energy to its vibration.

The installed driver was custom wound with 28 AWG copper wire to 5.1 Ω DC resistance. The supplied drawing shows the 20 × 20 mm body, M5 attachment, and 200 mm leads.

The build notes place a 5.6 Ω, 2 W resistor in series with a 100 nF capacitor across the coil. This small branch gives very high-frequency current a resistive path, helping the amplifier work with a coil rather than a loudspeaker.

Related work: Andrew McPherson's magnetic resonator piano, and the electromagnetically prepared piano developed by Per Bloland, Steven Backer, and Edgar Berdahl at CCRMA in 2005–06. McPherson later designed its updated amplifier board. The earlier system is described in Berdahl, Backer, and Julius O. Smith's ICMC 2005 paper.

Nine strings,
shared harmonics.

The central string carries my body weight. Its pitch is the reference for the other eight strings. I used their shared overtones to design places where the sounds could meet.

One string, many vibrations.

A string moves in several ways at once: along its whole length, in two halves, in three thirds. Each pattern has its own pitch. These pitches are called harmonics.

If the whole string vibrates 49 times a second, its halves vibrate 98 times, and its thirds 147 times. The feedback can bring one of these higher pitches into the foreground.

Different strings can share a pitch.

Just intonation means tuning with simple whole-number relationships. For example, string 2 is tuned to 6/13 of the centre's pitch. Its thirteenth harmonic meets the centre's sixth at a target of 294 Hz. I used these meetings as bridges between the strings.

This was the framework I used to design the system. The ratios meet exactly in the calculation; physical strings only approach them. Tuning and holding a long wire to 0.001 Hz is extremely difficult. Temperature, settling, the clamps, and wire stiffness all change what actually sounds.

The workshop notes record cuts, recuts, and adjustments during installation. The speaking length is the part free to vibrate, which can be shorter than the full piece of wire. Shortening it raises the pitch. Those notes are not a complete measured spectrum of all nine installed strings, and the layout dimensions in “Mass to pitch” do not reproduce every final adjustment.

Meeting, then drifting apart.

When two strings bring out the same pitch, they can blend. If the pitches are slightly different, the sound swells and fades as their waves move in and out of step. A larger difference can sound rougher. The filters keep changing which pitches we hear, so these relationships keep changing too.

In the design, strings 6 and 8 have eighth harmonics at 196 and 196.4 Hz. Their 0.4 Hz difference makes one slow pulse every 2.5 seconds. A small tuning change alters that pace.

Further explanation: UNSW's illustrated guide to harmonics and timbre.

The installation at night

It ran for the two-month exhibition. With no one in the room, the building joins the instrument: creaks, clicks, and the rain outside enter the field of feedback and harmonics. This recording was made with an ORTF pair of Neumann KM184 microphones in the centre of the space.
