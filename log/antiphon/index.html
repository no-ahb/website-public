How this version relates to the installation

The original engine assigns material to fourteen discrete speakers, combines three playback speeds, and measures its own output in six frequency bands. This browser instrument uses up to six overlapping voices, gentle fades, and stereo panning. Filtered random motion searches the map; measured brightness nudges the next selection toward a different spectral region. Holding a position keeps the same material in circulation.

The spatial view shows timbral coordinates, not speaker positions. It uses the available listening excerpts and omits the original engine's pre-composed sketches, long-form density cycles, and fourteen-channel routing. The finished composition includes Noah's subsequent editing and selection.

Last September, I recorded a string quintet playing through an orchestra of objects (cymbals, gongs, piano frames, sheets of metal and paper) with extended-range microphones that record above the limit of human hearing. I analyzed and mapped the recordings and wrote a program to traverse the map in real time for a 14-speaker room at the Haus für Medienkunst from March through May 2026.

Part I: Recording

We had one evening at Studio Richter Mahr to set up before the musicians arrived for two days. Chris Kalcov masterfully engineered an immaculate recording and re-amping system for over 32 channels on a Neve 5088 (RME Horus / MADI) at 192 kHz, operating at the very limit of what the equipment and the room could manage. I recorded with four musician friends and played violin myself.

We ended up recording 10 hours of audio over two days, or about 700 GB. At night, Chris played back the recordings from the day through the objects and re-recorded the result.

The instruments were close-miked with extended-HF Schoeps capsules and Coles 4038 ribbons; the bass with a Sennheiser MKH 800; the room with a Schoeps pair, a DPA 4006A, and an AEA R84 ribbon; the objects with Schoeps, a Neumann M49, and a Sennheiser MKH 20 on the bass drum. At 192 kHz the files (ideally!) reach to 96 kHz. The extended-HF capsules carry real content to roughly 40–80 kHz, which we were able to verify with spectrograms and pitch shifting.

The instructions for the session were broad and open, relying on the improvisatory intuition of players from classical, free-jazz, noise, and traditional-folk backgrounds.

Part II: The Corpus

The corpus consisted of 1,615 files, many of them as long as 45 minutes. The material I cared most about was ultrasonic, which meant slowing the audio to a quarter speed to bring it into the audible range, quadrupling the duration.

Trying to build systematically, take by take, quickly became overwhelming. So instead I turned to a process-based approach, thinking of the recordings as raw material and the act of composing as subtractive. In contrast to my installation work, which finds sound at the end of a long road of trial and error, this began with sound, structured by subtraction, layering, and transposition.

I organized the corpus by timbre, creating an aleatoric program to move through that multidimensional space. Each speaker was a single “voice”, clustered near other speakers by timbre, but sounding a distinct take or microphone perspective. So you might expect that the mic recording the brass sheet would be timbrally very similar to the same moment in time through the steel sheet, or through a cymbal. Speaker 1 would then play back the recording of the brass while speaker 2 would play the same instant through the steel. But then maybe a totally different moment in time turns out to be timbrally similar to another — or one material is unexpectedly similar to another (e.g., paper and a bass drum) — and the corpus traversal starts to bridge and fracture through the material in more interesting, web-like ways.

Part III: Process

First, I analyzed the material. I sliced the corpus into 303,799 five-second windows (2.5-second hop) and extracted 34 descriptive features per window (spectral centroid, flatness, flux, contrast and rolloff; pitch and a custom pitch-confidence; 13 MFCCs; a 20–75 Hz “roughness” measure; and an ultrasonic-energy ratio computed at the native sample rate). The analysis pass ran about 12 hours.

A separate rule-based pass flagged the windows that were really between-take talking — count-ins, discussion — so the map wouldn’t fill up with speech (about 3.8% of windows).

Everything was recorded at 192 kHz, and 72% of the files had real content above 20 kHz; the presence of ultrasonic energy tells the program to pull that material out on its own.

To make that 34-dimensional space walkable, I used UMAP, which collapses those features to a 2-D map you can look at. I fit UMAP on a stratified 100k subsample and .transform()-ed the remaining 203k onto it (n_neighbors=15, min_dist=0.05). Part IV: Playback

In the original explorer, a Flask backend serves the UMAP coordinates to a Plotly WebGL scattergl front-end that renders all 300k points at interactive framerates. Click any point and it streams that 5-second window back, decimated on the fly with scipy. Select a region to explore its timbral character. The archive becomes something you explore by ear. (A separate binary export of the same coordinates feeds the SuperCollider engine.)

Once I had a feel for the map, I wrote a generative engine to play it back — about 4,000 lines of SuperCollider, specific to the fourteen-speaker room at the Haus für Medienkunst. It went through four rewrites. Long story short, the structure is a continuous drone with pre-composed sketches that surface out of it and dissolve back in. The drone is the ocean; the sketches are the waves.

A cursor drifts across the map by filtered Brownian motion, with a drift speed (from calm to storm), a range (stay local or roam), a path smoothness, and a “gravity” that pulls toward dense regions; every step pulls new five-second windows to play across the speakers. Each window can play at three speeds: 1x, 0.5x, or 0.25x. At full speed it’s recognizable instruments and music; at half speed it becomes more textural and uncanny; at quarter speed it becomes grain, ultrasonics, hiss, and the sound of the recording chain.

The engine listens back to itself for some awareness of its state: a small analysis synth taps its own output, splits it into six bands, and reports the spectral centroid and spread. That drives a homeostasis loop so the field never collapses or fully stagnates.

sig   = In.ar(in, 2).sum * 0.5;        // tap the engine's own master output
bands = [ BPF.ar(sig, 40, 1.5), BPF.ar(sig, 155, 1.4), BPF.ar(sig, 548, 1.3),
          BPF.ar(sig, 1732, 1.15), BPF.ar(sig, 4899, 1.02), BPF.ar(sig, 12000, 0.83) ];
amps  = bands.collect { |b| Amplitude.kr(b, attackTime, releaseTime) };
amps.do { |amp, i| Out.kr(analysisOut + i, amp) };   // → control buses the language reads

In the end, I found the map too unpredictable to trust for an unattended two-month run. It had long stretches of uninspired wandering — doubling back, lingering in place — interspersed with flashes of musical genius. So I recorded long passes of the engine running, listened for about two days, curated the 5–8 minute chunks I particularly liked, and reassembled them in Reaper with a few pre-composed chordal sequences from the sessions. The result is a hybrid of machine and human composition. Listen to the finished recording.
